Why FEA results can be misleading

A converged solution is not automatically a correct engineering answer.

Convergence is a numerical statement. It says the solver found a solution that satisfies the discretised equations to a tolerance. It says nothing about whether those equations describe the physical problem in front of you.

The common failure pattern is familiar: a model runs, the contour plot is smooth, stresses look plausible, and the result is accepted because nothing obviously went wrong. But a model can converge perfectly and still represent the wrong load path, the wrong constraint, or the wrong failure mechanism.

Three questions before a result is trusted

  • Is the load path right? Where the load actually enters and leaves the structure is usually decided by contact, preload and joint behaviour — not by the geometry alone.
  • Is the constraint real? A convenient symmetry plane or a fixed bolt face can suppress exactly the deformation that is causing the problem.
  • Is the quantity being read the quantity that matters? Peak nodal stress at a re-entrant corner is not a physical stress. It is a mesh-dependent number.

Each of these can be wrong while the solution still converges cleanly. The plot looks the same either way.

What makes a result evidence

A result becomes evidence when it has been challenged: mesh and convergence checks, sensitivity to boundary conditions and material inputs, equilibrium and hand-calculation sanity checks, and — where it exists — comparison against measurement.

The question is never “does it converge?” It is “what would have to be true for this answer to be wrong — and have we checked?”

That is the difference between running a simulation and doing engineering with it. The contour plot is the beginning of the argument, not the end of it.

What makes a simulation trustworthy?

What verification and validation can establish — and what they cannot.

Trust is not a property of a model. It is a relationship between a model, a specific use, and the evidence available to support that use.

Two distinct questions are usually mixed together. Verification asks whether the analysis has been set up and solved correctly — geometry, loads, boundary conditions, material inputs, mesh, convergence, numerical behaviour. Validation asks whether the model represents the real system adequately for its intended use.

What verification can establish

  • The equations being solved are the equations you intended to solve
  • The solution is numerically converged and stable against discretisation
  • The model satisfies equilibrium and passes hand-calculation checks
  • The conclusions are — or are not — sensitive to the uncertain inputs

What validation adds

Validation compares prediction against something independent: a component test, field measurements, strain data, vibration response, operating behaviour. The comparison is only meaningful if the intended use is stated first. A model validated for trend prediction has not thereby been validated for absolute life prediction.

What neither can establish: that the model answers a question nobody defined. Verification and validation confirm fitness for a stated use — they cannot tell you whether the use itself was the right one.

Confidence is graded, not binary

Our evidence ladder makes this explicit: assumed input, calculated result, simulation result, measured result, validated result, released design. Confidence increases as independent evidence increases — and not every project needs to reach the last rung. Stating where a result actually sits is part of the engineering.

A model is trustworthy when its limits are as well documented as its results.

When nonlinear analysis is actually necessary

How to decide when the physics requires nonlinear behaviour and when a simpler model is better.

Nonlinear analysis is not a higher level of engineering. It is a specific response to a specific physical nonlinearity. The method should follow from the behaviour the answer depends on.

Four questions that decide it

  • Does the load path change with load level? If contact opens, closes or slips during loading, the structure is a different structure at each increment.
  • Does the material respond proportionally? Yielding, plasticity, hyperelasticity and damage mean stress is no longer proportional to strain.
  • Do displacements change the loading? Large deformation alters geometry, equilibrium and — in buckling problems — whether equilibrium survives at all.
  • Does history matter? Fatigue, preload sequences, cyclic plasticity and impact all depend on what happened before the current step.

If the answer to all four is no, a linear model is not a compromise. It is the correct, proportionate tool — and it is easier to verify.

When simpler is better

A nonlinear model multiplies the ways to be wrong: contact definitions, convergence parameters, load stepping, material characterisation. Every one of those needs evidence. A complex model that cannot be verified does not outperform a simple model that can — it merely moves the uncertainty somewhere less visible.

Often the right sequence is staged: an independent hand calculation to establish scale, a linear model to find the critical region, then nonlinearity introduced only where the physics demands it.

The objective is not the most complicated model. It is a model and evidence base that are appropriate for the decision.

Sometimes the right conclusion is that a more complicated analysis is not necessary. Saying so is part of the job.
